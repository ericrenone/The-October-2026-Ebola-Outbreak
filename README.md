# The 2026 Ebola Outbreak: Where Things Stand

**A complete guide to the epidemic in the Democratic Republic of the Congo, the cases in Kenya and Nigeria, and what is being done about it.**

ERI Labs | Emergent Reality Intelligence | Jersey City, New Jersey

**Last updated: 29 October 2026** · Day 167 since declaration · Day ~250 since the first estimated infections

---

## The short version

An Ebola outbreak has been spreading in the eastern Democratic Republic of the Congo since it was declared on 15 May 2026. On 27 October the Congolese health ministry reported **10,141 confirmed cases and 4,928 deaths**, from data current to 26 October. Just under half of everyone confirmed infected has died.

It is now the **largest Ebola outbreak ever recorded in Congo**, across all seventeen of the country's outbreaks and all species of the virus. It is the **second largest in the world**, behind only the 2013–16 West Africa epidemic, which infected more than 28,000 people and killed more than 11,000. And it is the **fastest-growing Ebola outbreak ever recorded anywhere**: at the equivalent point in the West Africa epidemic, about 4,000 people had been infected.

Three things make it unusually difficult.

The **virus**. This is Bundibugyo virus, a rarer species than the one almost every Ebola vaccine, drug and test was built for. There is still no licensed vaccine and no approved treatment for it anywhere in the world.

The **place**. The affected provinces hold an active armed conflict, more than a million displaced people, and a health workforce that has repeatedly gone unpaid. A nurse responding to an Ebola alert was killed by a crowd on 24 October.

The **blind spot**. Roughly **80% of new cases are being found outside the chains of transmission the response is monitoring**. Africa CDC's threshold for an outbreak that is actually under control is the reverse: at least 95% of new cases should come from people already on a contact list. The response is still finding infections after they have spread, not before.

October also brought the first real international spread. **Kenya** has recorded nine cases and two deaths, all traced to one imported patient who died in Nairobi on 5 October. **Nigeria** confirmed a single imported case on 20 October — a Swiss woman who had been on holiday in Uganda, not Congo — and evacuated her to Geneva on 26 October. Genomic work now links the Kenyan and Nigerian viruses to each other and to the Congolese epidemic, and leaves open an uncomfortable possibility: an undetected chain somewhere in Uganda, a country that declared its own outbreak over months ago.

There is real progress underneath all of this, and it is worth saying plainly. The **Tumaini vaccine trial** began in Bunia on 23 October — the first properly designed trial of a vaccine against this species of Ebola, anywhere, ever. Testing that could not detect this virus in May now works on machines already installed across the region. More than 2,700 people have recovered. On 22 October, the treatment centre in Mongbwalu — the mining town where the outbreak began — discharged its last confirmed patient and stood empty for the first time since May.

## Part 1: What this virus is

### "Ebola" is a family, not a virus

Four members of the orthoebolavirus family make people sick. The one most people have heard of — behind the 2014 West Africa epidemic and most outbreaks since — is **Zaire ebolavirus**.

This outbreak is caused by a different member: **Bundibugyo virus**, often shortened to BDBV. It was identified in 2007 and, before this year, had caused only two known outbreaks: Uganda in 2007, with a case fatality rate around 30%, and Congo in 2012, around 50%. Both were small. Neither reached a thousand cases.

This distinction sounds like taxonomy. It is the single most important fact about the outbreak, and here is why.

### Why the species matters so much

Almost every tool the world built for Ebola was built for Zaire.

**The vaccine.** Ervebo, the licensed Ebola vaccine, protects against Zaire ebolavirus. It is not licensed for Bundibugyo, and it is genuinely unknown whether it protects against it. There is laboratory evidence of partial cross-protection and animal evidence that is mixed. Anecdotal reports from this outbreak suggest vaccinated health workers who became infected have been less severely ill than unvaccinated colleagues — which is a hypothesis worth testing, not a finding.

**The drugs.** Inmazeb and Ebanga, the two antibody treatments approved for Ebola, were shown in a proper clinical trial to improve survival. Both work against the Zaire species. Neither is approved for this one. (Regeneron extended its US government supply contract for Inmazeb by about $270 million on 26 October — a stockpiling decision for future Zaire outbreaks, not a treatment for this one.)

**The tests.** The most widely installed Ebola test across central and east Africa looked for genetic sequences specific to Zaire ebolavirus. It did not reliably detect Bundibugyo. Early in this outbreak, patients who actually had Ebola tested negative, and confirming a case meant shipping a sample to Kinshasa, days away.

So the outbreak began with the world's entire Ebola toolkit pointed at the wrong target. That is the structural fact underneath everything that followed. The testing problem has since been fixed, and the vaccine and treatment problems are being worked on, but the first three months were spent fighting a virus the equipment could not see.

### How it spreads

Ebola spreads through direct contact with the blood, vomit, diarrhoea, sweat, saliva or other bodily fluids of someone who is sick or has died of it, or with surfaces and materials those fluids have contaminated. It is **not airborne**. A person is not infectious before symptoms begin.

Two activities therefore drive most transmission: **caring for a sick family member at home**, and **washing and preparing a body for burial** — a practice of deep cultural importance across the region, and the single most dangerous moment in an Ebola epidemic, because a body is at its most infectious just after death.

Transmission is also amplified in **health facilities with inadequate infection control**. Kenya's cluster is a textbook illustration: four of the eight secondary cases were health and care workers who treated the index patient before anyone knew what he had.

One further transmission route matters and is poorly understood by the public: **survivors can carry the virus in certain bodily fluids, including semen, for months after recovery**, and can transmit it sexually. WHO restated this in late October. Survivors do not transmit through sweat, saliva or casual contact once they have recovered and been discharged.

### How dangerous it is

The crude case fatality rate in this outbreak sits at **48.6%** on the latest national figures, and WHO's last formal count put it at 48.3% on 20 October — roughly one in two confirmed patients does not survive. For young children it is far worse: among confirmed cases in children under five, about **72%** have died.

Two caveats are worth holding onto.

First, during an active outbreak, fatality rates are rough. Some patients counted today are still in hospital.

Second, and more importantly, **mild cases are more likely to go undetected than fatal ones**. One reason people are not coming forward is that Bundibugyo infection can look unremarkable at first — fever, headache, body aches, the symptoms of malaria and typhoid and half a dozen other things that are endemic and mostly treatable in the region. Milder infections that resolve at home may never be counted at all. The true proportion of all infected people who die is probably somewhat lower than 48%.

Two numbers in that table carry most of the meaning.

**Ituri holds about 72% of all cases.** It is where the outbreak began, in the mining town of Mongbwalu, and it has had responders on the ground since May.

**North Kivu kills a far higher proportion of the people it infects** — 56% against Ituri's 47% — while holding under a quarter of the cases. On the most recent reporting day covered by European monitoring, North Kivu generated 58 of 96 new confirmed cases nationally, more than Ituri did.

Part of that fatality gap is real: fewer treatment beds, patients arriving later, more deaths at home. Part of it is a measurement artefact — a province that is finding fewer of its mild cases will have a severe-looking case mix and a worse-looking fatality rate. Both are happening at once, and nobody can currently say in what proportion.

**Nord-Ubangi is the eighth province**, confirmed in the third week of October with three cases and one death. Like Sud-Ubangi before it, it sits far to the west of the eastern bloc where the epidemic has been concentrated, which is why its appearance matters more than its case count.

**There are no confirmed cases in Kinshasa**, Congo's capital of some 17 million people. That remains the worst realistic scenario and has not happened.

### Why the numbers disagree

If you follow this outbreak across sources you will see different totals on the same day. This is not sloppiness, and it is worth understanding.

The Congolese government, WHO, the European health agency and the US CDC each publish figures on their own schedules, and they lag one another by days. On 25 October the national count was 10,060 cases and 4,887 deaths; two days later it was 10,141 and 4,928. WHO's most recent formal outbreak report, dated 23 October, carried data to 20 October: 9,704 and 4,687. All three are correct within their own systems.

Weekly trend numbers are shakier still. Different organisations count by date of symptom onset, date of report, or date of laboratory confirmation, and their weekly totals are not comparable. Treat any single weekly percentage you see about this outbreak with caution, including the ones on this page.

## Part 3: The shape of the epidemic right now

### The curve bent, then unbent

Late September produced the first genuinely encouraging data of the outbreak. Weekly cases fell to their lowest since mid-June. More than 21 days had passed without a new case in 18 health zones. Ituri's three-week trend was negative. Weekly deaths were down from their mid-August peak.

Then it came back. A WHO Africa report released on 21 October found that infections **rebounded in early October after falling in late September**, and described the recent evolution of the epidemic as suggesting **"renewed intensification of transmission."** The same report flagged escalating insecurity, continued population displacement and restricted humanitarian access as disrupting both surveillance and response, and raising the risk of infections going undetected.

That last clause deserves emphasis. When access shrinks, reported cases can fall for two opposite reasons: because transmission is falling, or because the response has stopped being able to see. The late-September decline and the early-October rebound are both consistent with a real change in transmission and with a change in what the system could observe. There is currently no way to separate the two from public data.

### Daily reporting, for scale

Recent daily situation reports give a feel for the rhythm:

- **20 October**: 67 new confirmed cases (Ituri 37, North Kivu 29, Tshopo 1), 41 deaths — of which **23 were community deaths** and 18 occurred in treatment centres. 21 patients discharged recovered.
- **26 October**: 96 new confirmed cases (North Kivu 58, Ituri 32, Tshopo 6), 46 deaths.

Between WHO's 8 October and 23 October reports, Congo added 976 confirmed cases and 482 deaths. Of those 482 deaths, **307 happened in the community and 175 in treatment centres** — a community-death share of about 64%.

That ratio is the most revealing single number in this outbreak, and it has barely moved all year. It was 66–70% in August. It was 60% in the first week of October. It is about 64% now. Four months of maximum international attention has moved it within the range of noise.

### Geographic spread versus geographic concentration

Both are happening.

- **66 of Congo's 178 health zones** have recorded a case. Fifty of those 66 have reported at least one case in the last 21 days; 16 have not.
- **Three health zones — Mandima, Beni and Bunia — account for the largest share of cases and deaths in the most recent 21 days.** Mandima and Bunia are in Ituri, Beni is in North Kivu.
- At the same time, the virus keeps finding new ground: a new health zone in Haut-Uélé, a new province in Nord-Ubangi, an entirely new country in Nigeria — all in the same ten-day window.

The epidemic is simultaneously burning hottest in a handful of places it has occupied for months and seeding itself into places with no experience of it. Those two facts call for opposite responses, which is part of why the response is stretched.

## Part 4: Kenya

### What happened

On 6 October, Kenya confirmed the first Ebola case in its history.

The index patient was a 40-year-old Kenyan man who had lived in Congo for years. He had been ill for roughly a month, with other underlying conditions, and had been treated at several Congolese hospitals without a diagnosis. He travelled overland to Uganda, boarded a flight at Entebbe, and landed in Nairobi on 3 October. He went to a private hospital in Nairobi, was isolated immediately in the emergency department, and was moved to a dedicated isolation facility. Samples taken on 5 October tested positive for Bundibugyo virus at two national laboratories. He died that evening and was given a safe and dignified burial the next day.

### What followed

Over the next two weeks, **eight more cases appeared, all epidemiologically linked to him**:

- **Four family members.**
- **Four health and care workers** who treated him — including a 49-year-old patient attendant whose job involved cleaning utensils and linens.

Every one of the eight was **already under quarantine as a known contact when symptoms began**. That detail is the difference between a cluster and an outbreak. None was discovered in the community; none infected anyone outside the cluster.

Kenya traced **135 contacts** and quarantined all of them. The first cohort completed its 21-day quarantine on 25 October and was released; around 60 contacts have now been discharged. Kenya's health ministry asked communities to welcome them home, stating plainly that they pose no risk of infection.

A **second death** was reported on 23 October, bringing Kenya to nine cases and two deaths.

### How to read it

Kenya's performance has two halves, and the honest assessment has to hold both.

**The detection half worked.** A man walked into a hospital with a febrile illness and was in isolation the same day, confirmed within 48 hours, buried safely, and his contacts were traced in full. By 22 October Kenya had screened more than 709,000 travellers and tested 461 samples across five laboratories. That is a health system functioning.

**The exposure half did not.** Four of the people he infected were the staff who cared for him in the window before anyone knew. That is not a Kenyan failure so much as a universal one — it is what happens with any novel presentation of a haemorrhagic fever in a general hospital — but it is why "a single imported case was contained" is an incomplete description of what happened here.

And the journey remains the real indictment. This man was visibly ill for a month. He crossed an international border by road, passed through a second country, boarded a commercial flight and cleared airport screening in a third — and was identified only when he walked into a hospital. **Border screening is a weak net.** It catches people with obvious fever at the moment they are checked. It does not catch someone chronically ill who takes paracetamol before a flight. What actually caught this case was a hospital with an isolation protocol and staff who recognised what they were seeing.

## Part 5: Nigeria, and the Uganda question

### The case

On 20 October, Nigeria confirmed an imported case in the Federal Capital Territory, Abuja. It was the country's first Ebola case since 2014.

The patient is a 42-year-old Swiss national, born in Uganda, married to a Swiss diplomat posted to Nigeria. The couple had spent about two weeks on holiday in Uganda. She arrived in Abuja on **3 October**, developed symptoms on **7 October**, sought medical care on **11 October**, and tested positive on **20 October**.

**She had not been to Congo.** Switzerland's foreign ministry told reporters she is believed to have been infected while travelling in Uganda.

On 26 October she was medically evacuated to Switzerland with her husband. She is being treated at Geneva University Hospital; he, asymptomatic, is in isolation in Zurich. Nigeria's health minister approved the evacuation and noted that she may still be infectious, so strict protocols were maintained throughout.

Nigeria is now monitoring **52 contacts** domestically — 54 by European tallies — with her husband counted as the 53rd and monitored by Switzerland. Forty-four people connected to the case were placed on a 21-day no-fly list. The national and state incident management systems were activated. Three European health agency experts deployed to Nigeria from 26 October. **Nigeria currently has no Ebola cases, but has not been declared free of the virus**: it is working through the 42-day countdown that follows the last case.

### Why this one is harder than Kenya's

Kenya's case had a clean story: a sick man left an epidemic zone and died in a hospital two days after arriving.

Nigeria's case does not. A woman who never went to Congo was infected somewhere during a holiday in **Uganda** — a country that declared its own Ebola outbreak over after a 42-day countdown, having recorded 20 cases and 2 deaths, most of them imported.

There are three possible explanations, and none of them is comfortable:

1. **An airport encounter.** Africa CDC noted that the first patients in both Nigeria and Kenya passed through **Entebbe International Airport on the same day, 3 October**. Officials have said CCTV showed no physical interaction between them. Ebola does not spread through shared airspace, so this explanation requires a contaminated surface or an unobserved contact, and it is a stretch.
2. **A missed chain in Uganda.** Someone infected, undetected, somewhere along a tourist route. This is the possibility that regional health authorities are most concerned about, and the genomic work does not rule it out.
3. **An exposure nobody has reconstructed yet.** Investigators from several agencies are still working on her movements.

Whichever it is, the implication is the same: **a person can now acquire this virus outside the Democratic Republic of the Congo**, in a country with no declared outbreak, and the first anyone knows about it is a positive test two weeks later in a third country.

That is a different risk picture than "an epidemic in eastern Congo with occasional exported cases." It is also, bluntly, the scenario that every travel-restriction regime built around a Congo-only lookback period does not cover.

## Part 6: What the genetics say

On 28 October, scientists from the four affected countries jointly reviewed **1,126 viral genomes** from this outbreak, in an analysis convened by Jean-Jacques Muyembe, the Congolese virologist who co-discovered Ebola in 1976.

The findings:

- The **Kenyan and Nigerian cases belong to the same transmission chain**, and that chain traces back to viruses circulating in eastern Congo.
- Nigeria's own sequencing, done by the national disease control agency with the genomics institute at Redeemer's University, found the Nigerian virus **differs from Kenya's index virus by a single mutation**.
- The analysis **leaves open the possibility of an undetected chain in Uganda**.

Nigeria's health minister has been careful about what this does and does not prove: genetic relatedness establishes that the viruses are close cousins, not that one person infected the other. There is no evidence the Kenyan patient travelled to Nigeria.

What the sequencing does establish is that this is **one epidemic, not several**. The cases in Nairobi and Abuja are not independent spillovers from bats. They are the eastern Congolese epidemic, extended by air travel.

Two older genomic findings still hold and are worth repeating, because they are the most reassuring facts available:

- A study published in August established that the 2026 virus is **genetically distinct from the 2007 and 2012 Bundibugyo outbreaks** — a new jump from an animal reservoir, most likely bats, not a resurgence of an old strain.
- Phylogenetic analysis dated the common ancestor of sequenced cases to around **late February 2026** — nearly three months before the outbreak was declared — and found **no evidence that the virus is becoming more transmissible or more dangerous**. Diversity is increasing as expected; no variant is taking over; the mutations found are the ordinary signature of human-to-human transmission. If you see a claim that this virus is mutating into something worse, it is not supported by the published evidence.

## Part 7: Ituri and North Kivu — one virus, two trajectories

### Mongbwalu emptied

In the week of 20 October, the treatment centre run by Doctors Without Borders in **Mongbwalu** — the mining town in Ituri where this outbreak began — discharged its last confirmed patient and had **no confirmed cases in beds for the first time since May**. Footage of staff singing and dancing in the compound circulated widely on 29 October.

That is worth sitting with. The place where it started is, for now, clear.

It is also not the same as Ituri being clear. Ituri still holds 72% of all cases nationally and still generated 37 of 67 new cases on the most recent day WHO reported in detail. Mandima and Bunia remain among the three worst health zones in the country over the past three weeks. One emptied treatment centre in one town is a real milestone and a very partial one.

### Butembo filled

North Kivu's story runs the other way. Between May and 22 October, **Butembo alone recorded 1,472 confirmed cases and 735 deaths**. Earlier in October, MSF described its work there as fighting a megafire; the treatment centre at Kitatumba has 29 beds for a city of roughly two million people, and for weeks has had to turn confirmed patients away.

North Kivu's share of new national cases rose from 24% at the end of August to around 40% in early October, and on some recent days it has exceeded Ituri outright. Its contact follow-up rate — 73.3% — is the worst of any affected province and is explicitly described by WHO as insufficient.

### Why the same virus produces two curves

The virus did not change between the two provinces. Four things differ:

**Time.** Ituri has had responders present since May. North Kivu's surge is barely two months old. Response density compounds.

**History.** North Kivu was the centre of Congo's 2018–20 Ebola outbreak, a response marred by documented abuses. People have not forgotten. An October survey by the International Rescue Committee found that **85% of respondents in North Kivu said distrust of community responders was keeping them from seeking care**.

**Capacity.** Ituri has treatment centres built over five months. North Kivu has beds being added faster than staff can be found to run them.

**Conflict.** Both provinces sit inside an active war, but North Kivu's is the more contested, with territory held by multiple armed parties and response teams routinely read as agents of one side or another.

The practical lesson, and it is the central finding of this update: **sustained, locally endorsed presence bends the curve, and it does so without a vaccine.** Ituri declined through September without any mass vaccination campaign, because leaders vouched for responders, responders kept showing up, and the province's governor took a dose on camera. That pathway is replicable. It is slower than a vaccine and it is the only lever currently proven to work on this virus.

## Part 8: Violence against the response

Late September and October were the worst stretch of this outbreak for attacks on the people fighting it.

- **22 September**: a local political official was beaten by residents in Butembo after taking part in Ebola awareness work, and later died.
- **26 September**: a worker at a screening post outside Butembo was shot dead.
- **27–28 September**: two Ebola response workers were killed in North Kivu.
- **30 September**: the Ebola transit centre in the Kigonze displacement camp near Bunia was destroyed by fire, set by soldiers searching for weapons. About 19,000 people fled the camp.
- **24 October**: a **nurse was killed and a colleague severely injured** in Bafwasende, a rural town in Tshopo province, while responding to an Ebola alert. They were attacked by local youths who accused them of contaminating water sources and spreading the virus.

The Red Cross counted 11 violent incidents against its volunteers, including a burned ambulance, in the first 100 days of the response alone.

WHO's director-general and its Africa regional director both condemned the Bafwasende killing; the UN's senior Ebola coordinator called for a full investigation and urged communities to let health workers do their jobs safely. The regional director's framing was precise: misinformation and fear must never fuel violence.

Two observations about this pattern.

**First, it is not one phenomenon.** The Kigonze fire was a national army operation, not community resistance. The Bafwasende killing was a crowd acting on a rumour about poisoned water. The Butembo shooting was something else again. Treating all of it as "community mistrust" obscures the fact that the response is operating inside a war involving multiple armed parties, including the government's own forces.

**Second, coercion begets it.** In August, authorities in Butembo created a task force drawn from pro-government militia fighters to protect health workers and facilities. By late September armed men were operating handwashing checkpoints on roads into the city, and a commander was reported — on a single source, still unconfirmed — to have threatened to forcibly hospitalise and whip non-compliers. Both local civil society and WHO flagged the obvious risk at the time: using armed men to enforce public health measures in a community that already distrusts the response deepens the distrust rather than reducing it. That is the same conversion, in the same province, that turned burial-team friction into a pattern of attacks in 2018–20.

## Part 9: Children

Children are bearing a share of this outbreak that the headline fatality rate conceals entirely.

As of 26 October, according to the joint WHO–Africa CDC incident management team:

- **2,705 confirmed cases in people aged 0–17.**
- **1,345 of those are children under five.**
- **The case fatality rate among under-fives is 72%** — roughly seven in ten, up from six in ten a few weeks earlier.
- Children make up **just over one in four confirmed cases but around one in three deaths**.

And on the family side, from UNICEF:

- **More than 1,900 children have lost one or both parents or caregivers, or been separated from them**, since May — 1,581 in Ituri and 322 in North Kivu as of late September. That works out to **nearly two children orphaned or separated every day**.
- Those figures cover only the areas where UNICEF works alongside provincial social affairs teams. The real number is higher.
- UNICEF-supported services have reached more than 90,000 people with psychoeducation and over 15,900 family members with home-based psychosocial support.
- Around a dozen nurseries operate for children whose caregivers are sick or dead, staffed in part by Ebola survivors. UNICEF wants one beside every treatment centre.

The under-five figure is the most severe statistic in this outbreak from any source. Part of the explanation is medical — small children tolerate fluid loss poorly and deteriorate fast. Part is diagnostic: infections in very young children are especially hard to detect, because they cannot describe their symptoms and the presentation is easily confused with malaria. Many die before Ebola is ever suspected.

**The funding picture for children specifically**: UNICEF is seeking $150.8 million for its Ebola response, of which $113.4 million is for Congo. About $40.1 million has been mobilised — roughly a third.

## Part 10: What is being done

This is where the picture is better than it was three months ago, and it is the part of the story that gets the least coverage.

### Testing: solved

The most widely deployed Ebola test in the region could not detect this virus. That was a catastrophic starting position, and it has been fixed.

- **2 July**: WHO authorised the first laboratory test specific to Bundibugyo virus, with a two-to-three-hour turnaround.
- **3 September**: WHO granted emergency use listing to a multiplex haemorrhagic fever panel that detects Ebola across all four species that infect humans — including Bundibugyo — plus Marburg, Crimean-Congo haemorrhagic fever and Lassa fever. Crucially, **it runs on machines already installed across central and east Africa** and returns results in about an hour.

The practical effect is that the region's existing laboratory equipment went from useless for this outbreak to usable, without buying new instruments. Congo now has roughly two dozen diagnostic laboratories working on this outbreak, and decentralised testing has cut turnaround times substantially.

Two honest limitations remain. There is still **no rapid finger-prick antigen test** for this virus — everything available needs a laboratory machine. And no systematic study of how well current tests detect the specific virus circulating this year has been published.

### Vaccines: the trial that did not exist in May

There is still no licensed or emergency-authorised vaccine for Bundibugyo virus. But on **23 October 2026, the first participant was vaccinated in Bunia in the Tumaini trial** — *tumaini* is Swahili for hope — and that is the single most important development of the month.

**What Tumaini is.** A **Phase 2/3 randomised, controlled, adaptive platform trial** evaluating the efficacy, safety and immunogenicity of vaccine candidates against Bundibugyo virus disease, **in contacts of confirmed cases**. It is led and co-sponsored by Congo's National Institute of Biomedical Research (INRB) and the University of Antwerp's Global Health Institute, funded through CEPI's Bundibugyo vaccine programme — backed by the European Commission, the US State Department, Portugal's Fund for Science and Technology, the Republic of Korea and CEPI's own core funding — with operational support from MSF and its research arm Epicentre, the Institute of Tropical Medicine in Antwerp, WHO and Africa CDC.

**What is in it.** The first candidate is **Ervebo**, the licensed Zaire vaccine, which WHO's vaccine advisory group recommended prioritising for exactly this evaluation. Preparations are under way to add the two Bundibugyo-specific candidates: **Moderna's mRNA candidate** and the **Oxford/Serum Institute of India ChAdOx1 candidate**. Because it is a platform trial, new candidates can join without starting over. Estimated primary completion is April 2027.

**Why it matters.** Ervebo has been given to frontline workers since late August on a compassionate-use basis, on the hope of cross-protection, with nobody able to say whether it works. Tumaini is the mechanism that converts that hope into an answer. If Ervebo protects, it can be deployed at scale immediately — the doses exist and the cold chain is built. If it does not, the world stops spending scarce doses on it and the Bundibugyo-specific candidates become the whole game.

**Vaccination so far.** Congo received an allocation of **70,000 Ervebo doses** in August: 50,000 for frontline and health workers, 20,000 for research. Vaccination began in Kisangani (Tshopo) in late August, reached Bunia on 19 September with the provincial governor taking the first public dose, and reached Beni in North Kivu on 16 October. By early October roughly **7,400 people had been vaccinated** — about 6,300 within the effectiveness study and around 1,000 outside it. The campaign is designed to cover around 50,000 frontline workers over six to nine months.

That pace is the constraint. Not demand: **the bottleneck is logistics and delivery, not persuasion** — a point worth holding against the common assumption that Congolese communities refuse vaccines.

### Treatments: three shots on goal

There is no approved treatment for Bundibugyo virus. There are three serious efforts to find one, and all three are enrolling.

**PARTNERS** (Platform Adaptive Randomised Trial for New and Repurposed Filovirus Treatments). Sponsored by WHO with Africa CDC, coordinated by Oxford's Pandemic Sciences Institute, led in-country by INRB with the Institute of Tropical Medicine in Antwerp, and delivered alongside MSF and ALIMA. First patient enrolled 2 July. It randomises patients to standard supportive care, **MBP134** (a two-antibody combination from Mapp Biopharmaceutical, engineered to work across Ebola species and protective in animals even a week after infection), **remdesivir**, or both. Target: 700–1,000 participants.

**Maftivimab**, added in October. Regeneron is putting its single most potent virus-neutralising antibody — the maftivimab component of Inmazeb — into PARTNERS as a monotherapy arm, on the strength of laboratory and non-human-primate data against Bundibugyo. Preprint data are now available. This is the first time a major commercial antibody developer has committed a product specifically against this species.

**EBO-PEP**, launched 14 July by INRB, France's ANRS/Inserm and ALIMA, in Congo and Uganda. It tests whether the **oral antiviral obeldesivir**, taken after exposure, can prevent illness in high-risk contacts — people who have been exposed but are not yet sick. Children, pregnant and breastfeeding contacts excluded from the main study are offered ten days of intravenous remdesivir under monitored compassionate use. Funded initially with €3.4 million from the European Commission's global health partnership and $1 million from Africa CDC.

A post-exposure drug that works would change the arithmetic of this outbreak more than anything else on this list, because it converts a contact list from a surveillance tool into a treatment tool.

### Capacity

- **Roughly 2,000 Ebola treatment beds**, up from about 1,000 in August, across **more than 50 treatment centres**.
- **About two dozen diagnostic laboratories.**
- Mental health and psychosocial support has been formally integrated into the response for patients and survivors.

The honest caveat, repeated since August and still true: **a bed without a nurse is not a treatment bed**. WHO's incident manager has named staffing, not beds, as one of the largest gaps in the response. See Part 11.

## Part 11: Money, and where it is not going

### What has been pledged

A great deal. At an international meeting convened in September, Africa CDC subsequently described roughly **$2.9 billion** in associated commitments, including a stated US contribution of about **$886 million**. In late October, Africa CDC's director-general gave a different cut of the same picture: governments have **pledged about $3 billion, of which more than $2 billion remains outstanding**. African countries have committed $120 million between them.

### What has been disbursed — and the $700 million question

On 15 October, Africa CDC reported that international donors have disbursed approximately **$962 million directly to organisations implementing the Ebola response in eastern Congo**.

Of that, only about **$200 million has been accounted for** to the Congolese authorities formally coordinating the response. The organisations involved include UN agencies — WHO, UNICEF, the World Food Programme — and international charities, as well as local groups.

Congolese authorities added a second fact that reframes the first: they have **received no money directly from international donors**. The funds flow to implementers, not through the national system that is nominally in charge.

Africa CDC's position, stated publicly, is that it is unacceptable for organisations to receive hundreds of millions of dollars during a major health emergency without providing a transparent account to the national authorities responsible for coordinating the response.

There are reasonable defences of the current arrangement. Bypassing weak national financial systems is standard humanitarian practice; implementers have their own audited reporting lines; "unaccounted for" in a national ledger is not the same as "missing." But the political effect is not in dispute, and it is corrosive. The phrase that circulates locally in eastern Congo is that **"Ebola is a business."** A public, unresolved dispute over $700 million between the continental health agency and the agencies spending the money is the single most effective possible validation of that belief, and it arrived at the exact moment the response most needs people to walk into treatment centres.

### The thing that still has not been fixed

Congolese health workers responding to this outbreak have gone unpaid.

Strikes have recurred since July across multiple treatment centres. Workers in Bunia described going two months without pay, with outstanding payments for the first 45 days of the response and unpaid transport allowances covering 75 days. In one town, staff saw hazard pay cut from $25 a day to $12. The government began disbursing bonuses at the end of July and has said repeatedly that payments are being processed, attributing delays to verifying which workers were actually deployed. MSF, which runs several treatment facilities, has said the protests concern government compensation rather than its own payments, and that health workers should be compensated promptly for work under extremely challenging conditions.

Fifty health workers have died of this virus. Those who remain have been asked to treat a disease that kills half the people who catch it, intermittently without pay, in a province where crowds have killed their colleagues.

**Clearing those arrears would cost a rounding error against $962 million already disbursed.** It has not happened in the five months it has been obviously necessary. It is the cheapest, highest-leverage, most persistently unmade investment in this entire response, and that has been true in every ERI Labs assessment since 7 August.

### The wider funding picture

The UN humanitarian appeal for Congo as a whole was **54% funded as of 21 October — $1.15 billion against $2.13 billion**. The ONE Campaign, flagging that figure, called on donors to increase funding, warned that inadequate protective equipment and unpaid wages have weakened containment, and asked that funding be transparently tracked and that the Ebola response not be allowed to cannibalise routine health services like childhood vaccination and maternal care.

That last point is not hypothetical. In Mongbwalu, first-dose measles coverage fell by 69% during the response.

## Part 12: Trust, and the surveillance gap

### The number that defines the outbreak

**Roughly 80% of new confirmed cases are arising outside the chains of transmission the response is monitoring.**

Africa CDC has stated the control threshold explicitly: **at least 95% of new cases must come from people already on a contact list** for an outbreak to be considered under control.

So the response is operating at roughly 20% against a 95% target. Supporting figures tell the same story from different angles:

- **Contact follow-up: 79.7% nationally** on the most recent full reporting day, against a required threshold of 95%. Bas-Uélé (98.9%) and Sud-Ubangi (97.3%) clear it. Ituri (92.8%) and Haut-Uélé (89.9%) are close. **North Kivu is at 73.3%** — described by WHO as insufficient — and accounts for nearly 20,000 of the roughly 30,000 contacts under follow-up nationally.
- **About 64% of deaths are still occurring in the community** rather than in treatment centres.

Put together: the response is following four-fifths of a list that captures a minority of the people it should contain, and is finding four-fifths of new patients outside that list entirely. **That is case-finding, not contact tracing.** Contact tracing works by knowing in advance who has been exposed, watching them, and isolating them the moment they become infectious. What is happening instead is discovering infections after the fact, usually after they have already spread.

### What people actually believe

Much reporting on Ebola in Congo emphasises conspiracy theories and resistance. That is real and documented. The data is more specific, and more actionable, than the impression.

**The October 2026 IRC survey** found that **85% of respondents in North Kivu said distrust of community responders was keeping them from seeking care**. That is the highest figure from any survey in this outbreak and it comes from the province with the worst fatality rate and the worst contact follow-up. It is not a coincidence.

**A nationwide survey fielded between July and September**, across all 26 provinces with 1,516 respondents, found a different and more hopeful national picture: 96% had heard of the outbreak; **74% said they would take a vaccine if one were available**; 88% said they would accept a trained burial team; 28% believed the virus was artificially created by foreigners; and only **20% knew that survivors can transmit the virus through certain bodily fluids well after recovery**. Health workers were the single most trusted information source. People holding the lab-creation belief were three times more likely to refuse vaccination.

**The classic study**, from the 2018 outbreak in Beni and Butembo, found 25% believed Ebola was not real, a third thought authorities had fabricated it for financial gain, and fewer than a third trusted local authorities — but health workers were rated more trustworthy than government officials.

Three conclusions follow.

**The demand problem is not what it appears.** Set 74% stated willingness to be vaccinated against roughly 7,400 people actually vaccinated out of a 50,000-person target. The constraint is delivery.

**The knowledge gap is specific and fixable.** One in five people knows that survivors can transmit the virus after recovery. That is not a conspiracy belief; it is an information deficit, and it drives both stigma against survivors and real transmission risk. WHO's public clarification in late October — survivors can transmit sexually, but not through sweat or saliva — is exactly the right intervention and should be repeated until it lands.

**Mistrust where it exists has reasons.** Responders who work this problem locate it in history, not irrationality: outside institutions arriving during crises and leaving afterwards, documented abuses during the 2018–20 response, and the lived observation that teams arrive for Ebola but not for malaria or typhoid — the diseases that kill people there every year. One aid worker's framing is worth borrowing: in areas where the response has established a longer-term presence, it works better; the problem is that the virus keeps reaching new health zones where trust has to be built from scratch, inside conflict zones where armed groups actively sow disinformation.

**The deepest measure of trust in this outbreak is not any survey.** It is that roughly two-thirds of deaths still occur at home. People are voting with their feet.

## Part 13: The international picture

### Formal status

WHO declared this outbreak a **Public Health Emergency of International Concern in mid-May 2026** — its highest formal alarm, the same designation used for COVID-19 and the 2014 West Africa epidemic. The emergency committee advising on that status has met twice, most recently in August, concluding that the outbreak remains an international emergency but does not meet the criteria for the higher "pandemic emergency" category. Updated temporary recommendations issued on 24 August emphasise coordinated control, cross-border collaboration and sustained surveillance.

**WHO's formal risk assessment**, last revised on 14 August, rates the risk as **very high in Congo, high for countries sharing a land border with Congo, and low for the rest of the African region and globally**. That assessment is currently being revised in light of the Kenyan and Nigerian cases.

**The European health agency** assesses the likelihood of infection for people from the EU/EEA living in or travelling to affected areas as low, and for people living in the EU/EEA as very low. Four cases have been medically evacuated or imported to the EU/EEA since May — two to Germany, one to the Netherlands, one in France — all involving people working in healthcare or humanitarian roles. A UK resident was evacuated precautionarily in July. The Nigerian case and her husband went to Switzerland in October.

**WHO continues to advise against any restriction of travel to, or trade with, affected countries.** Several countries are doing it anyway.

### Uganda

Uganda has gone hardest. Having closed its own outbreak — 20 cases, 2 deaths, mostly importations from Congo — it has now **banned all non-essential travel to and from Congo**, and requires its own citizens returning from Congo to **quarantine for 21 days at their own cost**. Its health minister framed the measures as keeping Uganda free of Ebola.

Those measures were announced against the backdrop of the Nigerian case, which appears to have been acquired in Uganda. There is an unresolved tension there that the country has not publicly addressed.

### The United States

The US has built the most expansive travel regime of any country, in three layers:

1. **Since May**: foreign nationals who have been in Congo within the prior 21 days are barred from boarding US-bound flights. Those restrictions were extended to US citizens in July, with arrivals routed to designated airports for screening.
2. **Also in force**: foreign nationals who have been in **Uganda or South Sudan** in the prior three weeks are barred.
3. **From 23:59 Eastern on 3 November 2026**: **every passenger and crew member on every international flight to the United States** must sign a declaration attesting they have not been in Congo in the prior 21 days.

The attestation order was signed on 22 October by the Assistant Secretary for Health. It applies regardless of nationality or departure point, covers commercial flights, charters, private jets and general aviation, and includes passengers merely transiting a US airport. One form per passenger; families cannot share one. Airlines must verify every attestation before boarding, confirm compliance to the government, refuse boarding to anyone without one, and retain the forms for at least 42 days. Exemptions are narrow — essentially government-authorised medical evacuations of Ebola patients and individual CDC grants. A Dublin–Boston flight requires it. A Tokyo–Los Angeles flight requires it.

**On 27 October, the airline industry asked for it to be reconsidered.** The International Air Transport Association, which represents over 370 airlines, and Airlines for America, which represents United, American, Southwest and Delta, wrote separately to the CDC. Their arguments:

- **The timeline is unworkable.** Changes to check-in systems, digital signatures, station procedures and staff training cannot be completed in under two weeks.
- **The scale is disproportionate.** More than 10 million passengers fly to the US each month. In 2025, US border authorities screened about 130 million air passengers; **fewer than 25,000 had any connection to Congo.**
- **The scope is arbitrary.** IATA asked why maritime and surface travel were excluded.
- **Exemptions are needed** for crew, US military personnel and jumpseat travellers.

An honest reading: the attestation closes a specific, real gap — the Kenyan index case cleared airport screening while chronically ill, and the Nigerian case was infected outside Congo entirely. It also imposes a documentation burden on 10 million people a month to find a population of a few thousand, and the Nigerian case is a direct demonstration that a Congo-only lookback would not have caught her. Both things are true.

### Elsewhere in the region

Nigeria has strengthened airport surveillance and begun a 42-day watch. Kenya has designated a single arrivals gate at its main airport for flights from ten countries at the centre of the outbreak, with health declaration forms, and has stood up isolation facilities near the Uganda border. South Sudan, the Central African Republic, Rwanda, Burundi, Tanzania and Congo-Brazzaville all have preparedness activity and no cases. The continental preparedness plan launched by Africa CDC and WHO in June ran a six-month window that closes in November; no revised version has been published, though cases have roughly doubled since it was written.

## Part 14: A note on measles in the United States

This is a separate situation, but it belongs in any honest account of how outbreaks are going in 2026, and it is a useful comparison.

| | |
|---|---|
| Confirmed US measles cases in 2026 (CDC, to 22 Oct) | **4,588** |
| Total for all of 2025 | 2,289 |
| Jurisdictions reporting | 47 |
| Separate outbreaks | 43 |
| Share unvaccinated or vaccination status unknown | 96% |
| Pennsylvania cases (state data, 28 Oct) | **1,398** across 44 counties |
| Pennsylvania hospitalisations | 265 |
| Pennsylvania cases among vaccinated people | 4 (under 1%) |

The United States has **doubled last year's full-year total**, which was itself the highest since 1991. Pennsylvania alone accounts for nearly 30% of national cases and is the epicentre; vaccination there has surged in response, with more than 39,000 doses administered in the first three weeks of October against a monthly average of 25,000.

**The death count remains the uncomfortable part.** Pennsylvania's health department has confirmed five measles-associated deaths this year; some reporting puts the state's toll at six. The CDC lists three nationally, does not specify where they occurred, and describes the figure as subject to change as reviews complete. The CDC changed its death-counting method in September and is working with state epidemiologists toward a standardised case definition whose effect, critics argue, would be to exclude deaths Pennsylvania has confirmed.

Both numbers are defensible within their own systems. A federal dashboard showing three while a state reports five or six is nonetheless corrosive to public confidence regardless of which methodology is more rigorous — and that is exactly the kind of small institutional friction that, scaled up and sustained, produces the trust problems described in Part 12.

The comparison with Congo runs in one direction only. Measles is far more contagious than Ebola — among the most contagious diseases known — but it has a safe, cheap, extremely effective vaccine that has existed for sixty years. **Congo is fighting a disease with no vaccine. The United States is losing ground against one it solved decades ago.**

## Part 15: The ERI Labs framework, updated

### Why the old model needed replacing

ERI Labs has tracked this outbreak since April with a model built around a single quantity: a **trust score**, scaled 0–9, computed from institutional history, recent behaviour, cultural respect and information speed, with a collapse threshold at 2.5.

That model earned its keep. It correctly predicted that contact tracing would degrade, that community deaths would dominate, that information campaigns from distrusted sources would backfire, and that Uganda's high-trust system would contain importations that Congo's could not. It also made two errors that matter.

**Error one: it treated vaccination as the only circuit-breaker.** Ituri's curve bent in September with no mass vaccination campaign at all. The model said that was impossible before 2027.

**Error two: it ran at national resolution.** A single Congolese trust score of 2.7 describes no actual place. Ituri and North Kivu produced opposite curves from the same virus in the same month.

The September update fixed the second error by going provincial. October exposed a third problem that a trust score alone cannot capture: **trust is necessary and not sufficient.** Kenya has high institutional trust and still lost four health workers to one patient, because the first exposure happened before anyone knew what they were looking at. Nigeria has high trust and a 2014 containment legacy, and still took thirteen days from symptom onset to diagnosis. Trust explains why people come forward. It does not explain whether the system can see them, hold them, or close the loop behind them.

### The new framework: the Containment Chain

**Containment is a chain of four gates. Every infection has to pass through all four for the response to get ahead of it. The gates multiply.**

| Gate | The question | Observable proxy | National value now |
|---|---|---|---|
| **1. Seen** | Was this infection already known to the system before it transmitted? | Share of new cases arising from existing contact lists | **~0.20** |
| **2. Reached** | Did the person get to care alive and early enough to matter? | Share of deaths occurring in treatment centres, not the community | **~0.36** |
| **3. Held** | Was there a staffed bed, and did the patient stay in it? | Staffed capacity against weekly caseload | **~0.75** |
| **4. Closed** | Were the contacts listed and followed to day 21? | Contact follow-up rate against the 95% standard | **~0.84** |

**Containment Index C = Seen × Reached × Held × Closed**

National value, late October 2026: **0.20 × 0.36 × 0.75 × 0.84 ≈ 0.045.**

Reading of the index:

| C | Regime | What it looks like |
|---|---|---|
| **above 0.50** | Containment | The response gets ahead of transmission; the curve bends within weeks |
| **0.25 – 0.50** | Contested | Containment possible in some zones, not others; curve plateaus |
| **0.10 – 0.25** | Case-finding | The response treats and buries but does not prevent |
| **below 0.10** | Chasing | Reported cases reflect detection effort more than transmission |

The point of a multiplicative index is that **it punishes the weakest gate and refuses to let strong gates compensate.** This is the structural reason the outbreak has absorbed $962 million, 2,000 beds, two dozen laboratories and a working test without bending. Gates 3 and 4 have improved enormously since July. Gate 1 has barely moved. Twenty per cent on the first gate caps the whole system at 20%, no matter what the others do.

It also explains the apparent paradox of the autumn: Congo's capacity metrics and its epidemic curve have moved in opposite directions. They are not in contradiction. They are different gates.

### What drives each gate

Each gate has a dominant input, and they are not the same input. This is the part the single trust score was flattening.

| Gate | Dominant driver | Secondary driver | Lever that moves it |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Seen** | Trust | Clinical suspicion | Sustained local presence; leader endorsement; case definitions that catch mild presentations |
| **Reached** | Trust | Distance and cost | Decentralised care; transport support; family presence protocols |
| **Held** | Payment | Beds | Clearing wage arrears; staffing, not construction |
| **Closed** | Security | Staffing | Humanitarian access; de-escalation; never coercion |

So: **trust governs the front half of the chain, money governs the middle, and security governs the back.** A response that is strong on one and weak on another does not get a weighted average. It gets the product.

### Provincial and national scores, 29 October 2026

Trust is retained as an input — it still drives the first two gates — and is now computed per territory, model estimates, 0–9 scale.

| Territory | Trust (0–9) | Seen | Reached | Held | Closed | **C** | Regime |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Uganda** | 8.2 | 0.85 | 0.80 | 0.85 | 0.95 | **0.55** | Containment |
| **Nigeria** | 7.8 | 0.70 | 0.75 | 0.90 | 0.95 | **0.45** | Contested–Containment |
| **Kenya** | 7.4 | 0.80 | 0.70 | 0.90 | 0.99 | **0.50** | Containment |
| **DRC — Ituri** | 3.6 | 0.25 | 0.40 | 0.80 | 0.93 | **0.074** | Case-finding |
| **DRC — Haut-Uélé** | 3.0 | 0.22 | 0.35 | 0.70 | 0.90 | **0.049** | Case-finding |
| **DRC — Tshopo** | 2.2 | 0.18 | 0.33 | 0.65 | 0.84 | **0.032** | Chasing |
| **DRC — North Kivu** | 1.1 | 0.13 | 0.30 | 0.60 | 0.73 | **0.017** | Chasing |

Changes since the September scores: **Ituri 3.3 → 3.6** (Mongbwalu emptied, Tumaini launched locally, leader-endorsed vaccination held). **North Kivu 1.2 → 1.1** (the IRC's 85% distrust figure, Butembo's bed shortfall, continued attacks). **Uganda 8.9 → 8.2** — the first downgrade Uganda has taken, on information speed, because a traveller appears to have been infected there and the first the world knew of it was a positive test in Abuja. **DRC national 2.7 → 2.9**, which continues to describe no actual place and is retained only for continuity. **US 6.2 → 5.8**, on the unresolved divergence between federal and state death counts.

### Three new entries in the ledger

**The accountability withdrawal.** The dispute over $700 million is a trust event with a specific mechanism, and it should be weighted like an abuse scandal rather than like a budget story. It does not merely fail to help; it supplies direct evidential support for the single most widespread local criticism of epidemic response — that *Ebola is a business*. Weight: **−0.5** on every affected province simultaneously, which is unusual; most withdrawals are local.

**The coercion conversion.** Militia checkpoints, forced hospitalisation threats and no-fly lists all convert a trust problem into a security problem. The ledger should record them at the same weight as an abuse scandal, because communities will remember them the same way. Weight: **−0.8** where present.

**The visible-recovery deposit.** This is new, and it is the one piece of genuinely good news in the model. An emptied treatment centre in the town where the outbreak began is social proof of the only kind that counts: people who went in came out. The 2,700 recoveries nationally are the same asset, mostly unused. Weight: **+0.4**, and it compounds if survivors are visible and destigmatised — which is exactly what the 20% awareness figure about survivor transmission is currently preventing.

### What the framework says to do

The index is multiplicative, so **the only rational strategy is to attack the weakest gate.** For Congo nationally and North Kivu especially, that is **Seen**, at 0.13–0.20.

Nothing on the current response menu moves Seen except sustained, locally endorsed presence — which Ituri demonstrated works, in about four weeks, without a vaccine. Everything else on the list improves gates that are already the strongest in the chain.

Which produces an uncomfortable ranking of priorities:

1. **Replicate Ituri's leader-mediated access model in North Kivu**, before the militia-checkpoint model becomes the province's permanent memory of this outbreak.
2. **Clear the wage arrears.** It is a rounding error against $962 million and it is the only lever that moves **Held** without building anything.
3. **Publish the money.** A reconciliation of the $962 million is a public-health intervention, not an accounting exercise.
4. **Make survivors visible.** 2,700 recoveries and 20% public awareness of post-recovery transmission is a wasted asset and an avoidable stigma, simultaneously.
5. **Finish Tumaini.** Not because a vaccine is the circuit-breaker — the model no longer believes that — but because not knowing whether Ervebo works is currently costing doses, credibility and planning capacity all at once.

## Part 19: What to watch

If you want to follow this outbreak without tracking every daily figure, four numbers tell you most of what is happening.

**1. The share of new cases arising from known contacts — currently about 20%.** This is the whole ballgame. Africa CDC's control threshold is 95%. Until this rises substantially, nothing else much matters, because the response is finding infections after they have spread rather than before.

**2. The share of deaths occurring at home — currently about 64%.** It was 66–70% in August and 60% in early October. If it falls and stays down, people are reaching care earlier and the central failure mode is easing. If it stalls, the rest is treading water.

**3. North Kivu's contact follow-up rate — currently 73.3%.** The worst in the country, in the province with the worst fatality rate, holding two-thirds of all contacts under follow-up nationally. This number is a direct readout of whether access is improving.

**4. Whether health workers get paid.** The cheapest, most obviously necessary and most persistently unmade investment in this entire response.

### What would count as good news

- The known-contact share rising past 40%.
- The community-death share falling below 50% and staying there.
- North Kivu follow-up clearing 90%.
- A Bundibugyo-specific candidate entering Tumaini.
- A first efficacy signal from PARTNERS, which would give clinicians the first proven therapy for this virus.
- Arrears cleared, strikes ended, and a published reconciliation of the $962 million.
- More emptied treatment centres, and survivors visibly welcomed back.

### What would count as bad news

- A confirmed case in Kinshasa, which would put the virus in a city of 17 million with international air links.
- Confirmed onward transmission in Kenya, Nigeria, Uganda or South Sudan outside a known cluster.
- Evidence of an established, undetected chain in Uganda.
- Further destruction of treatment facilities, from any cause.
- The under-five fatality rate staying near 72%.
- A second wave of health-worker strikes during the current rebound.

## Part 20: Where the record is unclear

Worth knowing, so you can read coverage of this outbreak critically.

- **Case totals differ between sources by more than a day's worth of cases.** Congo's health ministry, WHO, ECDC and the US CDC publish separate figures that lag one another. On 20 October WHO reported 9,704 cases; on 27 October the national count was 10,141. Both were current when published.
- **Weekly trend figures from different organisations are not comparable** and should not be combined. They count by different dates.
- **The two funding totals in circulation — roughly $2.9 billion and roughly $3 billion in pledges — cannot be cleanly reconciled** from public sources, and the $962 million disbursement figure has not been broken down publicly by recipient.
- **No reconciliation of money pledged against money spent has been published by anyone**, which is the substance of the current dispute.
- **The Nigerian patient's exposure has not been established.** The airport-coincidence hypothesis, the missed-Ugandan-chain hypothesis and an unreconstructed exposure all remain open.
- **No Ugandan contact-tracing figures have been published** for either the Kenyan index case's road and air transit or the Nigerian case's two-week stay.
- **The date Uganda's outbreak was declared over is reported variously**, from late July to late August; the underlying facts — 20 cases, 2 deaths, a completed 42-day countdown — are consistent across sources.
- **Health-worker infection counts lag badly.** The most recent firm figure is 50 deaths, reported in early October; infection totals were last given in detail in August.
- **The province-level tables in this document mix cut-off dates** between 20, 24, 25 and 26 October, because no single source publishes all of them on the same day. Totals will not reconcile exactly.
- **One provincial contact-follow-up figure in WHO's 23 October report (Tshopo) is internally inconsistent** and has been omitted from the analysis here rather than reconciled.
- **US measles death counts differ between CDC (three) and Pennsylvania (five, with a sixth reported).** Both are correct within their own methodologies.
- **The allegation of whipping and forced hospitalisation by militia enforcing quarantine in Butembo comes from a single source** and remains unconfirmed.
- **No peer-reviewed survey of community attitudes in the worst-affected provinces in 2026 has been published.** The IRC's 85% figure is the best available and comes from an organisational survey, not a peer-reviewed study.
- **Trust scores and Containment Chain values in Part 15 are model estimates, not measurements.** They are published so they can be argued with.

## How to use this

**For responders.** The weakest gate is *Seen*, and only sustained, locally endorsed presence moves it. Ituri proved that in about four weeks without a vaccine. Replicate it in North Kivu before the checkpoint model becomes the province's memory of this outbreak. Pay the arrears.

**For funders.** Earmark for salaries, not only beds. A bed without a nurse is a trust withdrawal: people see the building and the empty ward. And publish the reconciliation — at this point, accounting for the $962 million is itself an epidemiological intervention.

**For media.** The North Kivu story is not "Ebola surges." It is that the same virus produced opposite curves in two provinces of the same country, and the variable was not the virus. Report the 80% figure as the headline number, because it is.

**For anyone tracking this.** Compare Ituri and North Kivu week by week. If the two curves converge downward, leader-mediated access won. If they converge upward, the response ran out of road. That comparison is the whole thesis, live.
